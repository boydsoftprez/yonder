What does the browser get?

The bench note settles something architecture.md got wrong: the tee is free — 68% of a core for one output, 70% for two — but each consumer that leaves over cellular costs its own bitrate. A ground station pull, a browser watching and an RTP push are three copies of the same picture up one link: 6 Mb/s for one camera, against a field LTE uplink that is often 1–5. So this is not a detail, it is the difference between video that works in a field and video that works on a desk. Uplink measured at 3.2 Mb/s in all three.

1 — The browser gets the same encode as the ground station

Simplest, and it is what the split design does by default. One encode, two consumers, same bitrate each. On a link with room it is perfect. On a 3.2 Mb/s uplink, opening the console while a ground station is receiving puts you 25% over and both pictures fall apart — and the operator's only clue is that everything got worse at once.

2 — The browser always gets a small, cheap encode

Ground station2.0 Browser 640×3600.4
second encode off the decoded frames · ~3% of the board

The browser copy becomes almost free. The expensive part of this pipeline is the JPEG decode, and it is already paid — a second scaled encode reuses those frames and the hardware encoder is measured at four points. 640×480 encoding is 3% of the board, so this buys back 1.6 Mb/s of uplink for a few CPU points. The cost is that you can never see full quality in the console, and checking focus on a long lens through a 640 px preview is exactly the moment you would want to.

You get the cheap picture for flying and the real one for checking, and the page tells you what the second one costs before you ask for it. The preview runs at 640×360; Full quality switches the browser onto the ground‑station encode for as long as you hold it, and the annunciator says in advance that doing so needs 4.0 Mb/s of a 3.2 Mb/s link. That is R‑VID‑11 doing real work rather than reporting a number — "so it can be chosen rather than discovered" is the M4 line, and this is what choosing looks like.

Two consequences worth naming. The adaptive ceiling should be capped to the measured uplink less whatever the preview is taking, so the two controls stop fighting. And the picture is visibly soft in this mockup on purpose — an operator must never be able to mistake a 640 px preview for the footage the aircraft is actually sending, so the badge stays on the picture the whole time it is the cheap one.

The stacked track is one instrument, not three numbers: each output is a segment, the white mark is the measured capacity of the path they leave by, and anything past it is hatched red. That is what R‑VID‑11 asks for — the total against the capacity — and under R‑UI‑09 a bounded quantity cannot be a bare figure anyway.
